## SCORM section: Introduction to EDA (lesson 3 of 12)

**Byline:** Eric Shantz. The screen defines event-driven architecture (EDA) as a software design pattern in which decoupled applications asynchronously publish and subscribe to events through an event broker. It lets information flow in real time among applications, microservices, and connected devices instead of waiting for periodic polling.

### What is an Event?

The course calls everything that happens within or to an enterprise an event, including customer requests, inventory updates, and sensor readings. The value of knowing about and reacting to an event degrades over time. Faster delivery of information to the places that need it can help a business retain a customer, shift production, or reallocate resources. The examples are a customer purchase and shipping containers being loaded. ![Payment terminal illustrating a customer purchase event](img/event-example-customer-purchase.gif) ![Cargo ship and loaded containers illustrating a shipping event](img/event-example-shipping-containers.gif)

EDA pushes information as events happen, rather than waiting for periodic polling as in the API-led approach the screen says most companies use today. When an event occurs, EDA sends information about it to all systems and people that need it. An event may move through applications written in different languages, using different APIs and protocols, to endpoints such as applications, analytics engines, and user interfaces. The original diagram shows a point-of-sale system publishing an “Order 44334” event through a central event broker to Financial Management, Inventory Management, and Human Resources systems. ![Order 44334 flows from a point-of-sale system through an event broker to financial, inventory, and human-resources systems](img/pos-event-fanout-to-business-systems.gif)

### If events cannot reach their destination

The expanded panel says connected “things” cannot connect, relied-on applications and systems fail, and people across the company cannot react to situations requiring their attention.

### Benefits of Event-Driven Architecture

The screen presents four interactive tabs. Their full text is:

- **Responsiveness:** Since everything happens as soon as possible and nobody is waiting on anyone else, EDA provides the fastest possible response time.
- **Scalability:** Because you do not have to consider what is happening downstream, you can add service instances to scale. Topic routing and filtering can divide services quickly and easily, as in “command query responsibility segregation.”
- **Agility:** To add another service, have it subscribe to an event and generate new events of its own. Existing services do not know or care that this happened, so it has no impact on them.
- **Flexibility:** An Event Mesh lets services run in the cloud, on premises, or in another country. Since the Event Mesh learns where subscribers are, services can move without the other services needing to know.

The screen adds that these advantages matter when one change can have large downstream consequences. Real-time information and quickly adding services and analytics can improve business processes and customer experience.

### FAQ carousel content visible on the opening screen

- **Slide 1 — Frequently Asked Questions:** “Some extra information you might want to know!”
- **Slide 2 — What is meant by “Loose Coupling” or “Decoupling” of applications?** By introducing a middleman known as an Event Broker, Event-Driven Architecture enables Loose Coupling of applications, meaning applications and devices do not need to know where they are sending information, or where information they are consuming comes from. Loose Coupling does not come without some difficulties, but can dramatically improve system agility and scalability. In EDA, an event is sent without the expectation of anything in the way of a reply, except optionally an acknowledgment from the event broker. The benefit is that there is no dependency between sender and receiver; they are Decoupled.
- **Slide 3 — Where does an Event Broker fit in?** An Event Broker is middleware (software, an appliance, or SaaS) that routes events between systems using the Publish-Subscribe Messaging Pattern. All applications connect to the broker, which accepts events from senders and delivers them to all systems subscribed to receive them. Good system design and governance help ensure events reach the places they are needed, along with effective communication between event senders and those who need to respond. Event Portal tooling can capture, communicate, document, and govern the event-driven architecture.
- **Slide 4 — Where does an Event Portal fit in?** Organizations adopting EDA can find it difficult to document the design process and understand how system changes affect other parts. Event Portals let people design, create, discover, catalog, share, visualize, secure, and manage events and event-driven applications. Architects use the portal to define, discuss, and review events, data definitions, and application relationships. Developers use it to discover, understand, and reuse events across applications, lines of business, and external organizations. Data Scientists use it to understand event-driven data and discover insights by combining events.

### Primer Part 1 video and slide content

The embedded video is “Distributed Computing Primer - Part 1,” with a browser-reported duration of about 9 minutes 35 seconds. It has no text tracks, captions, or transcript link. I started playback at 2x; playback is still in progress. The downloadable original `3_Distributed-Computing-Primer.pdf` gives the visible course-slide content, recorded here as slide notes rather than a transcript.

A distributed system has multiple applications and computers working together to appear as one service or function. Its components communicate to coordinate actions and share state/data, and may be physically deployed in different locations. The course gives three reasons: horizontal rather than only vertical scaling; better fault tolerance and high availability; and improved responsiveness by getting closer to users. Its single “How” point is effective communication between processes/computers.

The course lists three communication objectives: trigger or signal another process to perform work; pass the necessary data/state to that process; and support distribution at “any scale.” File-based communication lets Process 1 write files to a shared/networked disk and Process 2 read them. It passes data/state through file contents, but triggering requires polling filesystem changes, and any-scale distribution is limited by shared-disk access such as NFS or workarounds such as FTP between servers. Database-centric communication passes data/state through table records, but triggering requires polling for record changes; although network-accessible and more flexible than files, the database becomes a protected resource that can limit connectivity and scale. Network socket-based communication passes data/state over the socket, and the arrival of data can trigger activity; “Internet scale” is possible subject to network reach.

The next listed objectives are to shield application developers from socket-programming code and decouple applications from other processes’ location, availability, and pace. The linked original slides preserve the course diagrams: concentric communication layers; file and database write/read flows with objective assessments; and direct socket communication with all three objectives marked successful. ![Original course diagram peeling from distributed computing to distributed communications](img/distributed-computing-layers.png) ![Original file-based communication diagram showing shared-disk writes and reads and its objective assessment](img/file-based-communication.png) ![Original database-centric communication diagram showing table-record exchange and its objective assessment](img/database-centric-communication.png) ![Original network socket communication diagram showing socket writes and reads with all three communication objectives met](img/network-socket-communication.png)

## SCORM section: Topic Taxonomy (lesson 8 of 12)

The opening screen is by Eric Shantz and introduces the topic after the earlier discussion of Publish/Subscribe and event taxonomy: “Now let’s talk more about Topics.” The timeline explains the roles and addressing model:

1. **Publisher:** An application or service that creates and sends events, like a broadcaster. At RealStore, an item return at a POS creates a Return event and an Inventory Update event.
2. **Subscriber:** An application that wants events, like a listener tuned to a channel. The Accounting System subscribes to Return events; Inventory Management subscribes to Inventory Update events.
3. **Topics:** Events carry metadata describing the event, called a Topic. A topic is a designed addressing system analogous to a postal address. Forward slashes separate hierarchical levels, with each level adding specificity. Example: `retail/menswear/return/v1.2/{location}/{store_id}/{product_id}`. The page describes this as a refund transaction at a specific POS terminal and store.
4. **How publishers and subscribers communicate:** Publishers send events to topics. The Event Broker checks the topic’s subscriptions to determine recipients.
5. **Why this works well:** A publisher sends once to one topic; the broker can deliver to one or thousands of subscribers. Subscription-based filtering gives each subscriber the exact events it needs and keeps filtering logic in subscriptions rather than business logic.
